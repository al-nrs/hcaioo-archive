Before You Buy AI for Prior Authorization, Check the 2027 Rules

A procurement decision for hospital leaders, not another blanket claim of regulatory readiness.

Augmented Leadership HQ

A hospital can buy an AI prior authorization tool for the wrong deadline.

CMS's current guidance separates the payer requirements beginning in 2027 from the hospital reporting measure: for eligible hospitals and critical access hospitals, electronic prior authorization is an optional bonus measure in 2027 and mandatory from 2028. That is a meaningful procurement distinction, not a reason to stop preparing. Current CMS guidance.

We recommend funding a working authorization process before funding an AI promise. The purchase should solve a demonstrated bottleneck in your payer and service mix, not turn several different policies into one sales deadline.

Three clocks, three different decisions

The payer API clock. CMS-0057-F requires affected payers to support electronic prior authorization for non-drug items and services, with API compliance dates generally beginning January 1, 2027. Exact dates vary by payer type. The affected groups include Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service and managed-care programs, and Qualified Health Plans on federally facilitated exchanges. This is not a requirement covering every commercial plan. CMS final-rule fact sheet.

The hospital reporting clock. The FY 2027 hospital payment final rule makes the Medicare Promoting Interoperability electronic-prior-authorization measure optional for the 2027 EHR reporting period and mandatory from the 2028 reporting period. Do not use the older 2024 fact sheet's hospital timeline as your current implementation brief. CMS FY 2027 final-rule summary.

The drug-policy clock. CMS-0062-P proposes extending requirements to drugs, with provisions that would begin October 1, 2027. CMS still identifies this as a proposed rule in the sources checked for this issue. Do not promise that the finalized non-drug framework already covers every medication workflow, or present proposed dates as settled obligations. CMS drug proposal.

Ask compliance to document applicability by payer, benefit, service and program. This is an operating distinction to verify locally, not a substitute for organization-specific legal advice.

Buy the missing capability, not a second interface

Start with your EHR vendor and payer partners. CMS explicitly asks providers to discuss integration capabilities, additional modules, implementation timelines and testing opportunities. CMS readiness guidance.

Then identify the actual gap. Does your team struggle to discover requirements, assemble documentation, transmit requests, reconcile responses, or manage exceptions? Those are different buying decisions.

In our proposed procurement test, an AI-generated summary is useful only if it reduces verified work without inventing chart facts or hiding missing evidence. A connection can transmit a request without making a clinical decision. A polished packet can still leave staff copying information into a portal. Neither capability establishes the value of the other.

Require the demonstration to continue beyond submission. Can staff see a request for more information, identify its owner, supply the missing material and track the subsequent response? Can they distinguish an acknowledgement from an approval? Can they retrieve the authorization's scope and expiration without searching another inbox?

Put five records beside the contract

The following is our proposed buying checklist, not a CMS reporting instrument. Keep the records short enough to review in the approval meeting.

1. Coverage record. Revenue cycle owns a list of payer products, benefits and services in scope, with each connection marked tested, planned or unsupported. Define the eligible workload before testing. Report supported eligible authorization episodes divided by all eligible episodes, using the same service window. Do not count one payer logo as proof that every product is connected.

2. Transaction record. IT and revenue cycle retain authorized test evidence showing requirement discovery, documentation, submission, response and exception handling. Link messages to a unique episode. Separate transmission success, payer decision and unresolved status. A failed attempt followed by manual completion remains a failed automated attempt, not an invisible exclusion.

3. Evidence record. Clinical informatics owns source traceability for every AI-produced clinical assertion. Record corrections, omitted required information and unsupported statements against all reviewed packets. Reviewers need access to the underlying authorized chart material. Require human verification before submission during the evaluation; do not let the tool manufacture evidence to satisfy a requirement.

4. Work record. Operations measures active staff minutes per eligible episode, including review, corrections, retries, calls and fallback. Sum work across staff without counting the same person's overlapping minutes twice. Compare like payer products, services and urgency groups. Show incomplete episodes and their age separately rather than dropping them when the observation window closes.

5. Economic record. Finance separates verified expense reduction or incremental collected contribution from released staff capacity. Subtract incremental cash costs once, including licenses, interfaces, implementation, training, support and monitoring. Report existing salaried effort separately as opportunity cost. Faster authorization is not automatically a paid claim, and a projected reduction in denials is not collected revenue.

Test the exception before the easy case

Consider an illustrative outpatient imaging request. The payer asks for more information after the initial submission. This is a proposed test scenario, not an observed hospital or vendor result.

Run the same authorized case through the current process and candidate workflow. Retain the original request, the missing-information response, the correction and the final status. Measure who did the work and how long the episode remained unresolved. If the AI supplies a fact absent from the record, it fails that part of the test even if the packet looks complete.

Define acceptance criteria before reviewing results. Include an unavailable connection, an ambiguous response and a manual fallback. Urgent care needs its own escalation route; do not infer safety from an average turnaround time. A small test can expose a broken handoff, not certify rare-event safety or universal payer coverage.

The 2024 rule also establishes decision timeframes for affected payers other than exchange QHP issuers: 72 hours for expedited requests and seven calendar days for standard requests, with those process provisions generally starting in 2026. These are not promises of immediate approval. CMS scope and timeframes.

The approval to make this week

Authorize a bounded evaluation with named payer products, services, owners, an end date and an exit clause. Expand only where the connection works, clinical evidence remains traceable, exceptions have accountable owners and the measured benefit supports the full cost.

If native EHR capabilities address the bottleneck, require a separate justification for the additional AI layer. If interoperability remains unproven, do not let a demonstration of document generation stand in for end-to-end readiness.

The board question is simple: Are we buying a demonstrated reduction in authorization work, or paying for a compliance claim we have not mapped to our obligations?
